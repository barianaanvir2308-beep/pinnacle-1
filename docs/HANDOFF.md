## 0. How to use this handoff (read first)

You are taking over an in-progress student game project. Please follow these rules:

1. **Read before you change anything.** The code is the source of truth for current behaviour. This document summarises it, but values may have changed since it was written. Check `autoloads/globals.gd` first.

7. **The hardware code has not been written yet.** The circuit is finished (§8). The owner said "code it later", so start the firmware and the game link only when asked.
8. **Do as much as you can without stopping to ask.** When something is unclear, choose the default suggested here, say which assumption you made, and continue.

## 2. The game as the team designed it (framework pillars)

| Pillar | What it means in play |
|---|---|
| **Modules matter, not only the Arduino** | Towers have HP. Worms bite them and Ransomware locks them. Damaged towers can be repaired. You defend both the core and your towers. |
| **Towers combine into systems** | There are three families: Offensive, Defensive/Control and Support. Control and support towers have to be worth building even though they deal little or no damage. |
| **Energy is processing power** | The currency is **Energy**. It comes from passive generation, an Energy Decoder tower (planned), virus kills and wave bonuses. It is capped at an **Energy Capacity** of 1000. **Emergency Overclock** is a risky burst of Energy. |
| **Every virus needs a different answer** | Each virus type has a counter tower that is clearly best against it. |
| **Viruses choose routes** *(the team marked this unsure)* | Different viruses go after different objectives: Basic heads for the Arduino, while Worm and Ransomware head for modules. |
| **Towers cannot fully block the path** *(unsure)* | At least one route to the Arduino must always stay open. |
| **The player steps in during waves** | You can change targeting priority, fire tower abilities and use Overclock while a wave is running. |
| **Input** | Keyboard and mouse. You drag or place towers onto the grid, and on-screen indicators tell you when to react. |

**Game loop:** pick a level, then get a build phase (place, upgrade or sell towers) with a 10 s countdown. The wave then runs, and you can use abilities, Overclock, retargeting and repairs. Clearing the wave pays a bonus. You repair, rebuild and go again, for 10 waves per level. The game is lost when the Arduino reaches 0 HP and won when all 10 waves are cleared.

### 3.2 Autoloads and architecture

* **Autoloads:** `Globals` (constants and data), `Events` (signal bus) and `ResourceManager` (Energy).
* `main.gd` creates the managers as child nodes at runtime. For example, `OverclockManager` is created in `main.gd` around line 73 and added under `managers`.
* Systems talk to each other through **`Events` signals**. These matter most for the hardware link:
  * `enemy_defeated(enemy_type, reward, position)`: fires on every kill.
  * `overclock_used(energy)`, `system_crashed()` and `system_load_changed(load, max)`.
  * `wave_started(n)`, `wave_completed(n, bonus)`, `level_selected(i)`, `game_over()` and `victory()`.
  * `energy_changed(amount)` and `base_health_changed(hp, max)`.

### 4.2 Emergency Overclock (the in-game "Overdrive")

Pressing **O** or clicking the on-screen Overclock button calls `OverclockManager.use()`:

* It gives **+150 Energy** at once and adds **+50 System Load**. Load runs from 0 to 100.
* The cooldown is **15 s**.
* Load decays by 1 per second during waves and 2 per second in the build phase.
* At **load ≥ 60**, towers attack 25% slower. At **load ≥ 90**, they attack 50% slower and passive income stops.
* If load reaches **100**, the system **crashes**. All towers pause for 3 s, the Arduino takes double damage for 10 s, and load resets to 60.
* **Unlock:** on Level 1 it unlocks from wave 3. On every later level it is available from the start.
* Useful methods for the hardware link: `can_use()` (unlocked, in play, cooldown finished, not paused), `cooldown_left`, `system_load`, `towers_paused()` and `is_unlocked()`. The node is in the group `"overclock_manager"`.

### 4.3 Towers available now

| Tower | Cost | Damage | Range (tiles) | Interval (s) | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Antivirus Cannon | 100 | 15 | 3.5 | 1.0 | All-rounder (reference: 0.15 DPS per Energy) |
| Data Spike | 225 | 80 | 4.5 | 2.5 | Heavy single hits that **ignore Rootkit armour**. Targets the strongest virus. |
| EMP Tower | 175 | 4 | 2.5 | 4.0 | Stuns every virus in range for 1.0 s. Crowd control. |

The Sniper Node, Quarantine Zone and Overclock Hub scripts still exist, but they are hidden from the build menu and waiting to be replaced (§5).

**Upgrades** follow a Bloons TD Battles-style model. Each tower has **2 paths × 3 tiers**:

* Tier costs are 0.5×, 1× and 3× the base cost.
* Once one path goes past tier 1, the other path is capped at tier 1.
* Tower HP grows with its highest tier: ×1.0, ×1.15, ×1.35, ×1.7.
* Some tier-3 upgrades add an active ability on F1–F5: Data Spike *Purge* and EMP *System Freeze*.
* The full trees are in `UPGRADE_TREES` in `globals.gd`, with the reasoning in `docs/scaling_and_upgrades.md`.

## 6. Design rules to keep

1. Offensive towers are priced at about **0.15 DPS per Energy (±10%)**. Control and support towers are priced by what they achieve (stun uptime, buffs, Energy produced), not by DPS.
2. Income should be roughly **40–45% from the system, 30–40% from kills and 15–20% from wave bonuses**. There is no interest on saved Energy.
3. Each virus type has a fixed speed. HP rises about 8% per wave, and the spawn interval is the main difficulty knob.
4. Every virus that attacks modules needs a counter tower.
5. Warn the player on screen before Ransomware locks and Trojan splits.

## 8. Hardware add-on: the plan

### 8.1 Goal

A small physical "control console" connected to the computer over USB, with three parts:

1. **16×2 LCD:** shows **how many viruses the player has killed**.
2. **Pushbutton:** pressing it **activates Overdrive**, which is the in-game **Emergency Overclock** (the same action as the **O** key).
3. **Status LED:** **stays on when Overdrive is ready**, **blinks during the cooldown** and is **off while Overdrive is locked or not in play**.

The keyboard and mouse controls keep working. The hardware is an extra input and display, not a replacement.

### 8.2 Status

| Part | Status |
|---|---|
| Circuit (Tinkercad) | ✅ **Done.** Built on a breadboard and checked in Schematic view. |
| Arduino firmware | ⏳ Not started. Wait for the owner to ask. |
| Godot ↔ Arduino link | ⏳ Not started |
| Real-world build | ⏳ Not started |

The Tinkercad design is **"Protocol Zero Hardware"**: https://www.tinkercad.com/things/1oj91T9Td18 (it may still show its old auto-name, "Swanky Sango-Robo", in some places).

### 8.3 Parts

| Ref | Part | Note |
|---|---|---|
| U1 | Arduino Uno R3 | Connected to the computer over USB, which provides both power and serial |
| U2 | 16×2 LCD with an I2C backpack | In Tinkercad it's the PCF8574 type at **address 0x20** |

### 8.4 Wiring (breadboard layout)

| From | To | Wire colour |
|---|---|---|
| Arduino **5V** | Breadboard bottom **+ rail** | Red |
| Arduino **GND** | Breadboard bottom **− rail** | Black |
| LCD **VCC** | + rail | Red |
| LCD **GND** | − rail | Black |
| LCD **SDA** | Arduino **A4** (direct) | Blue |
| LCD **SCL** | Arduino **A5** (direct) | Yellow |
| Arduino **D2** | Breadboard column 5 (top half). The button straddles the centre gap in columns 5–7. | Green |
| Button, other side (column 7, bottom half) | − rail | Black |
| Arduino **D9** (PWM) | Column 15 (top half) | Orange |
| R1 220 Ω | Bridges the centre gap in column 15 (top half to bottom half) | — |
| LED **anode (+, long leg)** | Column 15, bottom half | — |
| LED **cathode (−, short leg)** | Column 14, bottom half | — |
| Column 14 | − rail | Black |

Electrically:

* D2 goes through the button to GND. With `INPUT_PULLUP`, D2 reads HIGH when released and **LOW when pressed**.
* D9 goes through the 220 Ω resistor and the LED to GND. Writing D9 HIGH turns the LED on.
* The LCD is on the I2C bus (A4/A5) with 5 V power.

### 8.5 Behaviour spec for the firmware

**LCD layout** (16 characters per line):

```
Line 1:  KILLS: 0127
Line 2:  OVERDRIVE READY      | COOLDOWN 12s | LOCKED | SYSTEM CRASH | NO SIGNAL
```

**Kill count:**

* Default: count every `Events.enemy_defeated`, including Trojan Minis and Worm clones.
* Reset to 0 when a level starts (`level_selected`) and keep the count between waves.
* *Open question:* the team may prefer a lifetime total instead (§9).

**Button:**

* Debounce it for about 30–50 ms.
* Send one Overdrive request per press, on the falling edge (HIGH to LOW). Holding the button must not repeat it.

**LED** (driven by state the game sends):

| Game state | LED |
|---|---|
| Ready (`can_use()` is true) | Solid on |
| Cooldown (`cooldown_left` > 0) | Blinks at about 2 Hz |
| Locked (not unlocked yet, not in play, or paused) | Off |
| System crash (`towers_paused()`) | Optional: a fast blink at about 8 Hz |

**No game connected:** if no message has arrived for about 3 s, show `NO SIGNAL` on line 2 and turn the LED off.

**Important:** the Arduino never decides whether Overdrive can fire. It only sends a *request*, and the game checks `can_use()`. This keeps the game as the single source of truth.

### 8.6 Serial protocol (proposed)

Use USB serial at **115200 baud** with plain ASCII lines ending in `\n`.

| Direction | Message | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Arduino → PC | `HELLO PZ1` | Sent once after boot (handshake) |
| Arduino → PC | `BTN OVERDRIVE` | The button was pressed |
| PC → Arduino | `K <n>` | Kill count, e.g. `K 127`. Send it on every change. |
| PC → Arduino | `S READY` / `S COOLDOWN <sec>` / `S LOCKED` / `S CRASH` | Overdrive state. Send it on every change, plus every 1 s as a keep-alive. |

### 8.7 Godot side (to build)

Godot 4 has **no built-in serial port API**. There are two options:

* **Option A (recommended default):** a small **Python bridge**, `tools/hardware_bridge.py`. It uses `pyserial` to talk to the Arduino (on macOS the port looks like `/dev/cu.usbmodem*`) and relays the same text lines to Godot over **UDP on localhost**: for example, Godot listens on 127.0.0.1:9101 and the bridge listens on 9102. Godot uses its built-in `PacketPeerUDP`. This needs no native plugins and is easy to debug. Run it with `python3 tools/hardware_bridge.py`, after installing pyserial with `pip3 install pyserial`.
* **Option B:** a serial **GDExtension** from the Godot Asset Library. It works without an extra process, but it depends on a native binary for Apple Silicon and Godot 4.7, so check compatibility first.

**In the game, add a `HardwareLink` node.** Make it an autoload, or have `main.gd` create it like the other managers. It should:

1. Connect to `Events.enemy_defeated`, increment `kills` and send `K <kills>`. Reset the count on `Events.level_selected`.
2. Every frame, or whenever Overclock state changes, build the state from the `OverclockManager` (group `"overclock_manager"`) using `is_unlocked()`, `in_play()`, `cooldown_left`, `towers_paused()` and `can_use()`. Send `S …` when the state changes and once per second as a keep-alive.
3. When `BTN OVERDRIVE` arrives, call `overclock_mgr.use()`. This is exactly what the O key does, so all the rules (unlock, cooldown, System Load, crash) still apply. If `use()` returns false, flash the HUD message "OVERDRIVE NOT READY".
4. Fail safely: if there's no hardware or no bridge, the game runs normally with no errors. Log the connection once.
5. Add `tools/checks/check_hardware.gd`. It should fake incoming lines and confirm that the kill count and state messages are correct and that `BTN OVERDRIVE` triggers Overclock exactly once.

### 8.8 Arduino firmware requirements (to build)

* **Board:** Arduino Uno. **Libraries:** `Wire.h` plus an I2C LCD library.
  * On real hardware, the usual choice is `LiquidCrystal_I2C`.
  * In Tinkercad, check which LCD library the simulator accepts for the PCF8574 LCD. Its example sketches use the built-in library.
* **I2C address:** use `0x20` in Tinkercad. **Real modules are usually 0x27 or 0x3F**, so run an I2C scanner sketch first and set the address it finds.
* **Pins:** `BUTTON_PIN 2` (`INPUT_PULLUP`), `LED_PIN 9`, LCD on SDA A4 / SCL A5.
* Use **`millis()` timing everywhere**, with no `delay()` in the main loop, so the button stays responsive while the LED blinks.
* Read serial without blocking: collect characters until `\n`, then parse the line.
* Only rewrite the LCD when its text changes, to avoid flicker.
* Test it in the Tinkercad simulator first. Use the Serial Monitor to type `K 5`, `S READY` and `S COOLDOWN 10`, and to see `BTN OVERDRIVE` when you click the button.

### 8.9 Suggested build order

1. Arduino firmware tested in Tinkercad with a fake serial input typed into the monitor.
2. `hardware_bridge.py`, tested with the real board and a terminal.
3. Godot `HardwareLink` and its headless check.
4. A full playtest: kills go up on the LCD, the LED follows the cooldown, and the button fires Overdrive.
5. Build it physically, check the real I2C address, and tidy the wiring.

3. **Extra LCD info:** should line 2 also show System Load or the wave number?
4. **Hardware stretch goals:** a buzzer on Arduino Damage, or more buttons for abilities F1–F5?
