# Make Protocol Zero playable by ear

Good game sound design treats audio as gameplay information before decoration. Every event that matters gets a distinct, recognizable sound, and a priority system decides what the player hears, so the loudest sound does not automatically win. Overwatch's "Play by Sound" philosophy is the clearest documented model of this ([Audiokinetic](https://audiokinetic.com/en/blog/overwatch-game-audio-using-wwise-1)). For Protocol Zero, that means a mix where the core taking damage always cuts through, thirty towers firing read as "a lot of towers" rather than noise, and defenders and viruses sound different in kind: tonal and "authorised" for towers, noisy and glitchy for viruses. Godot 4.7 already has every building block this needs. There are buses with a hard limiter, `AudioStreamRandomizer` for variation, `AudioStreamPolyphonic` and player pools for voice control, and the 4.3-era `AudioStreamInteractive` and `AudioStreamSynchronized` resources for adaptive music. The one big trap is that bus effects silently stop working in Web exports. ElevenLabs' new SFX v3 model can produce the whole effects set cheaply, at 40 credits per second, so a full first pass fits inside the free 10,000-credit allowance. But the API still defaults to the deprecated v2 model, its output loudness varies widely, and Free-plan output cannot be used commercially and must carry attribution. Generate the final assets on a paid plan (Starter, $6/month) if the game might ever be sold.

## Good game audio answers "what matters right now?"

Overwatch's audio team built its mix on five pillars: hero voice-over, a clear mix, pinpoint accuracy, gameplay information and "Pavlovian response." Its guiding motto came from Walter Murch's "Dense Clarity, Clear Density" ([Audiokinetic](https://audiokinetic.com/en/blog/overwatch-game-audio-using-wwise-1)). The team tried HDR audio first and dropped it because, as Scott Lawlor put it, with HDR **"the loudest sound wins."** They replaced it with an importance system that boosts the greatest threats and dims everything else ([PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/how-overwatch-uses-sound-to-pinpoint-threats-you-cant-see)). They also made sounds carry state: Mercy's healing beam rises in pitch as the target's health rises. And they cut uninformative voice lines so that players feel they always had the information to avoid a "cheap death" ([PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/how-overwatch-uses-sound-to-pinpoint-threats-you-cant-see)). Translated to tower defense, the threat questions become: which virus is closest to the core, which one is a boss, what just leaked, and what did the player just do. Those events should outrank routine tower fire and basic deaths.

Variation is the second principle, and it matters more in tower defense than almost anywhere else, because the same towers fire for whole sessions. A playback-rate multiplier of 2.0 raises a sound one octave and 0.5 lowers it one octave. One semitone is about ×1.06. Small random pitch shifts add natural variation even when players don't consciously notice them, and rising semitone steps make a natural-sounding "combo" escalation ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/the-power-of-pitch-shifting)). Audiokinetic's UI guidance adds that frequent sounds need multiple variations and randomized parameters, and must be tested over long play, because a sound that works once can become grating ([Audiokinetic](https://www.audiokinetic.com/en/approaching-ui-audio-ui-design-perspective-2)).

UI sound has its own grammar. **Consonant, ascending sounds read as success; inharmonic, descending sounds read as failure.** "Locking into place" feels rewarding. For errors, Audiokinetic recommends removing or dulling the success layers while keeping the physical click, instead of using a harsh buzzer ("Nobody likes it when their game gives them tinnitus"). It also warns that heavy feedback on frequent resource gains annoys efficient players ([Audiokinetic](https://www.audiokinetic.com/en/approaching-ui-audio-ui-design-perspective-2)). For Protocol Zero, the "insufficient funds" sound should be a soft, dulled version of the build sound, paired with the cost text flashing red. The gold tick should be the quietest rewarding sound in the game.

For crowds of simultaneous sounds, middleware converges on the same toolkit. There are per-sound, per-category and global voice limits, priority values that decide which voices get stolen, and distance-based priority ([CRIWARE](https://blog.criware.com/index.php/2021/01/19/voice-limiting-in-atom-craft/)). Unreal adds a minimum **Retrigger Time** between plays of the same sound, volume scaling of older instances, and the resolution rules Stop Oldest, Stop Quietest and Prevent New ([Unreal Engine](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/sound-concurrency-reference-guide)). None of these sources publish tested cap values. The numbers suggested later in this report are starting points to tune by ear.

The documented mistakes are mostly about balance. Adam Bishop names three: important sounds mixed under ambience, option sliders that don't control what they claim to, and explosions mixed too loud. In his words, "Game audio should be mixed for living rooms, not movie theatres" ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/game-audio-mistakes)). The only formal numeric targets are loudness standards. They call for **-24 LUFS integrated (±2 LU) with a -1 dBTP ceiling for PC and console, and -16 LUFS for mobile and handheld** ([IESD](https://www.audiogang.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/IESD-Mix-Ref-Levels-v03.02.pdf)), measured over at least 30 minutes of representative play rather than single clips ([Designing Sound](https://designingsound.org/2012/07/30/video-games-and-loudness-standards-interview-with-sonys-garry-taylor/)). On accessibility, Xbox guideline 105 asks for independent volume and mute per category plus a mono option ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/105)). The Game Accessibility Guidelines suggest an extra slider for informative cues, which a blind Killer Instinct player calls "crucial to playing the game without sight" ([GAG](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/provide-separate-volume-controls-or-mutes-for-effects-speech-and-background-music/)).

## Tower defense rewards escalating music and a voice in the machine

Tower defense audio has two well-documented successes. The first is **adaptive music that tracks threat**. Plants vs. Zombies opens each level with a calm string-and-piano loop during setup. Drums "kick in" when the first wave arrives, and later waves swap in a "horde" variant that keeps the earlier material and adds percussion. In the Roof stage, a robotic synth foreshadows the final boss ([NCSU analysis](https://classapps.chass.ncsu.edu/com304/?p=2616)). Players notice when this is missing. PvZ Replanted shipped without the dynamic system, players complained they were "playing a watered-down version," and an update restored it along with a separate SFX volume option ([After-Strategy](https://after-strategy.com/en/plants-vs-zombies-replanted-dynamic-sound-is-back/)). This is a secondary source, so treat its details as plausible rather than confirmed. The second success is **a voiced in-world operator**. Defense Grid had no cutscene budget, so it told its story through Fletcher, an AI that talks to the player. Players praised "the talking AI," and the creative director said that "having someone cheering you on and appreciating what you're doing was really important" ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/au/defense-grid-the-beer-game-that-changed-a-genre)). A system-AI voice announcing "Wave incoming," "Core integrity 50%" or "Overdrive online" fits Protocol Zero's computer-core premise almost exactly. Kingdom Rush is a useful reassurance for a student team: its sound effects were made by a designer who also wrote the story and designed the levels ([Kodeco](https://www.kodeco.com/30636/top-developer-interviews-ironhide-game-studio)).

Public material on event-level tower defense SFX (leak cues, coin sounds, upgrade stingers) is scarce. The sound plan below is therefore design inference built on the sourced principles, not documented practice from shipped games.

### Cyberpunk works when the digital sounds dirty and human

The best-documented cyberpunk audio direction comes from Cyberpunk 2077. Its world was meant to sound "organic, natural, sometimes maybe dirty and raw, but not too sci-fi." The team recorded vintage cameras for servo details because they wanted "an analog feel for even our most futuristic guns" ([A Sound Effect](https://www.asoundeffect.com/cyberpunk-2077-sound/)). The composers used a cheap Soviet Polivoks synth (Przybyłowicz calls it "an *ugly* piece of machine") and a circuit-board synth, drew on '90s bleep techno and IDM rather than stock synthwave, and added electric cello and live drums for "a sense of humanity" ([Inverse](https://inverse.com/gaming/cyberpunk-2077-music-composers-interview)). The game keeps this readable through mixing. When combat music triggers, it removes the bass from ambience and in-world music, and voice lines sidechain the beds, "always targeting readability" ([A Sound Effect](https://www.asoundeffect.com/cyberpunk-2077-sound/)). Hacknet's electro/darksynth soundtrack (Carpenter Brut, The Algorithm) is a good musical reference for "hacking" drive ([Bandcamp](https://bossbattlerecords.bandcamp.com/album/hacknet)). Ruiner shows the value of mellow, Ghost in the Shell-style pads as contrast to the aggressive tracks ([Laced Records](https://www.lacedrecords.com/blogs/blog/seeing-red-ruiner-s-creative-director-on-how-style-can-precede-substance)).

For Protocol Zero, this suggests one organizing rule: **defenders are clean and tonal, invaders are noisy and atonal.** The player can then hear "good" versus "bad" without looking. Pitched rewards (gold, upgrades) should sit in the music's key. Bitcrush and distortion add a lot of harsh upper-mid energy, so keep them for short, rare threat events, not for sounds that fire every half second. Ground the digital palette with real recordings, such as relay clicks, hard-drive seeks, fan whirr, or the team's own Arduino piezo buzzer. That last one also links the in-game Overdrive to the physical button.

| Event | Sound concept (design inference) | Frequency and priority |
|---|---|---|
| Core hit | Unique short alarm tone used nowhere else, plus a low thump; rate-limited | Rare; highest priority, always audible |
| Low core HP | Slow repeating warning beep plus "panic" music layer | State; high |
| Boss spawn | Boss motif enters the music 5–10 s early (PvZ-style), then a modem-handshake "intrusion detected" stinger | Rare; very high |
| Overdrive (hardware button) | Charge-up whine into a wide electrical surge; brief duck of the rest of the mix | Rare; very high |
| Wave start / clear | "Incoming connection" alert, then the drum layer enters; resolving chord plus "scan complete" chime | Per wave; high |
| Build / upgrade / sell | Satisfying "lock-in" thunk; same thunk plus rising 3-note arpeggio; descending coin blip | Player-initiated; medium-high |
| Insufficient funds / invalid placement | Dulled build click or soft two-note descending blip, never a buzzer | Player-initiated; medium |
| Cannon-type tower | Punchy low-mid square-wave thunk with a noise burst; 3–4 variants | Very frequent; low |
| Fast "data spike" tower | Very short bright FM zip (fast pitch-down) | Very frequent; low |
| Basic virus death | Crunchy bitcrushed pop, like the sample rate collapsing | Very frequent; low; throttled |
| Worm / swarm enemies | One shared loop whose volume follows swarm size, not one sound per unit | Continuous; low |
| Gold gained | Pitched FM blip in key, rising with quick successive pickups; batched per tick | Very frequent; quietest reward |

Low-only sounds such as cannon booms need upper-mid content too. MacBook and phone speakers reproduce little bass, so test the mix on laptop speakers as well as headphones.

## Godot 4.7 has every piece; wire them through one autoload

### Buses and the mixer

Create buses **Master ← Music, SFX, UI** (plus Ambience or Voice if you add an operator voice) in the Audio panel. Godot saves the layout to `res://default_bus_layout.tres`. Players reference buses by name, so renaming a bus silently drops its players back to Master ([Godot audio buses](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/audio/audio_buses.html)). Put an **`AudioEffectHardLimiter`** on Master (default ceiling -0.3 dB) as a safety net ([HardLimiter ref](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_audioeffecthardlimiter.html)). The older `AudioEffectLimiter` is deprecated ([Audio effects](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/audio/audio_effects.html)). To duck music under important cues, put an `AudioEffectCompressor` on Music and set its `sidechain` to the bus that should trigger the ducking ([Compressor ref](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_audioeffectcompressor.html)). For the "muffled world" effect under the pause menu, put a disabled `AudioEffectLowPassFilter` on Music and SFX, not Master, so UI clicks stay crisp, and toggle it with `AudioServer.set_bus_effect_enabled()` ([AudioServer ref](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_audioserver.html)). Drive the volume sliders with `AudioServer.set_bus_volume_db(idx, linear_to_db(v))` (or `set_bus_volume_linear` on 4.4+), and look up bus indices with `get_bus_index()` instead of hardcoding them. Godot 4.8-dev adds a GUI theme sound bus setting, but **it does not exist in 4.7** ([ProjectSettings 4.7 vs master](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/godotengine/godot-docs/master/classes/class_projectsettings.rst)), so UI sounds need players explicitly routed to the UI bus.

### Positional versus global players

Use plain **`AudioStreamPlayer`** for music, UI and global events such as core hit, wave start and Overdrive ([AudioStreamPlayer ref](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_audiostreamplayer.html)). Use **`AudioStreamPlayer2D`** for tower shots and enemy deaths. These attenuate with distance from the listener, which is the screen centre by default. Defaults are `max_distance = 2000` and `attenuation = 1.0` ([AudioStreamPlayer2D ref](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_audiostreamplayer2d.html)). The global `audio/general/2d_panning_strength` defaults to 0.5, which is tuned for headphones ([ProjectSettings](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_projectsettings.html)). On a single-screen map, keep panning subtle so sounds at the edges don't feel lopsided, and leave max_distance near the viewport diagonal.

### Voices, caps and variation

Godot offers three ways to control voices. A player's `max_polyphony` (default 1) cuts the oldest voice when it is exceeded. A single player with **`AudioStreamPolyphonic`** (default 32 voices) can fire any stream through `play_stream()`, which takes a per-call bus and returns `INVALID_ID` (-1) when full. Or you can pool player nodes ([Polyphonic playback ref](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_audiostreamplaybackpolyphonic.html)). Use a polyphonic player for UI and global one-shots, and a pool of `AudioStreamPlayer2D` nodes for world sounds, since each voice needs its own position. For variation, wrap every frequent sound in an **`AudioStreamRandomizer`** resource holding 2–5 takes, with `PLAYBACK_RANDOM_NO_REPEATS` (the default), `random_pitch` around 1.05–1.1, and `random_volume_offset_db` around 1.5–3. Those tuning values are a starting point, not sourced figures. On 4.6+ you can set the jitter in semitones with `random_pitch_semitones` instead ([Randomizer ref](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_audiostreamrandomizer.html)). Godot does not cap a sound's total instances or throttle retriggers for you, so the AudioManager has to. A minimal version is shown below. The caps and cooldowns are unsourced starting values. Because the pool is round-robin, the oldest voice gets stolen when the pool is full.

```gdscript
# audio.gd — autoload "Audio"
extends Node
const CAPS := {&"tower_fire": 4, &"enemy_hit": 5, &"enemy_death": 4, &"gold": 2}
const COOLDOWN_MS := {&"tower_fire": 50, &"enemy_death": 40, &"gold": 60}
var bank: Dictionary = {}   # StringName -> AudioStream (usually an AudioStreamRandomizer .tres)
var _pool: Array[AudioStreamPlayer2D] = []
var _next := 0
var _last := {}

func _ready() -> void:
    process_mode = Node.PROCESS_MODE_ALWAYS          # UI/music keep working while paused
    for i in 24:
        var p := AudioStreamPlayer2D.new()
        p.bus = &"SFX"
        p.process_mode = Node.PROCESS_MODE_PAUSABLE  # world SFX freeze with the game
        add_child(p)
        _pool.append(p)

func play_sfx_at(id: StringName, pos: Vector2) -> void:
    var now := Time.get_ticks_msec()
    if now - int(_last.get(id, -100000)) < int(COOLDOWN_MS.get(id, 0)):
        return
    var active := 0
    for p in _pool:
        if p.playing and p.get_meta(&"id", &"") == id:
            active += 1
    if active >= int(CAPS.get(id, 8)):
        return
    _last[id] = now
    var player := _pool[_next]
    _next = (_next + 1) % _pool.size()
    player.set_meta(&"id", id)
    player.stream = bank[id]
    player.global_position = pos
    player.play()
```

The same manager should hold a polyphonic `AudioStreamPlayer` on the UI bus and save the slider values to `user://settings.cfg` with `ConfigFile`. When a slider reaches zero, call `set_bus_mute`, because `linear_to_db(0)` returns negative infinity. Batch kills in game code: when many viruses die inside a short window, play one "sweep" sound instead of a stack of copies.

### Adaptive music

Godot 4.3 added three music resources: `AudioStreamPlaylist`, `AudioStreamSynchronized` and `AudioStreamInteractive` ([Godot 4.3 release](https://godotengine.org/releases/4.3/)). **`AudioStreamSynchronized`** starts up to 32 stems at exactly the same time and sets each stem's level with `set_sync_stream_volume()`. That is vertical layering, the PvZ approach ([Synchronized ref](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_audiostreamsynchronized.html)). CRI's guidance on vertical layering adds three requirements: every combination of stems must sound good, loops must be seamless, and the highest-impact layer (drums) should enter last ([CRIWARE](https://blog.criware.com/index.php/2020/04/20/dynamic-music-with-adx2-part1-vertical-layering/)). One composer's hard-won lesson is to write the calm base layer first, because the base layer he wrote last sounded flat ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/adaptive-music-in-competitive-games)). **`AudioStreamInteractive`** handles horizontal state changes (build → wave → boss → victory/defeat). Transitions can wait for the next beat or bar, and `FADE_AUTOMATIC` is the recommended fade mode. Switch clips at runtime with `get_stream_playback().switch_to_clip_by_name(&"wave")` ([Interactive ref](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_audiostreaminteractive.html); [Playback ref](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_audiostreamplaybackinteractive.html)). An interactive clip can itself be a synchronized stem set, so the two combine. Bar-quantized transitions only work if the OGG/MP3 import settings have `bpm`, `beat_count` and `bar_beats` filled in ([OGG importer](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_resourceimporteroggvorbis.html)).

### Formats, pause and the Web trap

The Godot docs recommend **WAV for short, repetitive SFX and Ogg Vorbis for music and long sounds**. WAV is cheap enough on CPU that "hundreds of simultaneous voices" are fine. Mono is enough for many SFX, nothing above 48 kHz is useful, and reverb belongs on a bus rather than baked into files ([Importing audio](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/assets_pipeline/importing_audio_samples.html)). Since 4.4, WAV imports default to QOA compression, which the docs describe as having "much higher quality and the lowest memory usage" of the compressed options ([WAV importer](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/classes/class_resourceimporterwav.html)). When the tree is paused, players set to the default Inherit process mode pause and resume automatically. Players for UI and the pause menu therefore need `PROCESS_MODE_ALWAYS` ([Pausing games](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/scripting/pausing_games.html)). The biggest gotcha concerns Web builds for itch.io. Since 4.3, Web exports default to Sample playback, in which **AudioEffects, reverb and doppler are not supported** and positional audio "may not always work correctly." Switching to Stream playback restores effects at the cost of latency and crackling ([Exporting for the Web](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/export/exporting_for_web.html)). If the course submission is a browser build, do the ducking and pause-muffle by tweening bus volumes in GDScript, which still works, rather than with compressors and filters.

## ElevenLabs can voice the whole set for pocket change, if you pin the model

### API, models and cost

The endpoint is `POST https://api.elevenlabs.io/v1/sound-generation`, authenticated with an `xi-api-key` header. The body takes `text`, `duration_seconds` (0.5–30 s; null lets the model choose), `prompt_influence` (0–1, default 0.3), `loop`, and `model_id`. The response body is the audio file ([API reference](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/text-to-sound-effects/convert)). The capability page gives a 0.1 s minimum instead, so stay at 0.5 s or above to be safe ([Capability page](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/capabilities/sound-effects)). **Always pass `model_id: "eleven_text_to_sound_v3"`.** The API still defaults to v2, which is deprecated and will be removed on 1 March 2027. v3 follows multi-part prompts better, is "less likely to clip" on chimes and whistles, supports looping, and according to the official changelog is available to every workspace ([Models](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/models); [Changelog](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/changelog/2026/11/2)). One third-party article reports "Unknown model" errors on some accounts ([DataStudios](https://www.datastudios.org/post/elevenlabs-sfx-v3-sound-effects-model-elevencreative-elevenapi)), so if v3 is refused, fall back to v2 temporarily.

Cost is **40 credits per second when duration is set** ([Capability page](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/capabilities/sound-effects)). That makes the budget easy. Forty one-shots at about 1 s, three takes each, cost about 4,800 credits. Three 20-second ambience loops add 2,400. The total of roughly 7,200 credits fits inside Free's 10,000 credits per month and comfortably inside Starter's 30,000 ([Pricing](https://elevenlabs.io/pricing)). The constraints that matter more are format and concurrency. Free and Starter get 128 kbps MP3 through the API, while 44.1 kHz PCM needs Pro. Plans allow 2 (Free) or 3 (Starter) simultaneous requests ([Pricing](https://elevenlabs.io/pricing)). A batch script should therefore run requests sequentially to avoid 429 errors. The web UI offers a workaround: non-looping effects can be downloaded as 48 kHz WAV there, though loops come out as MP3 only ([Product docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/product/sound-effects/overview)).

| Parameter | Recommended setting for Protocol Zero |
|---|---|
| `model_id` | `eleven_text_to_sound_v3` (never rely on the default) |
| `duration_seconds` | Always set: 0.5–1.5 s for one-shots, 2–4 s for stingers, 10–30 s for loops |
| `prompt_influence` | About 0.6–0.8 for a consistent set (higher is more literal and less varied); lower only when exploring ideas |
| `loop` | `true` only for ambience, swarm and firewall hums |
| `output_format` | `mp3_44100_128` on Free/Starter, then convert; PCM needs Pro |

### Prompting

The official guidance is to use short, concrete descriptions of action, material, setting and atmosphere rather than broad categories, and to describe sequences in order with "then" ([Product docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/product/sound-effects/overview)). ElevenLabs publishes a vocabulary the model understands, which includes **impact, whoosh, one-shot, loop, drone, stem, braam and glitch** ([Capability page](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/capabilities/sound-effects)). "Glitch" and "drone" map neatly onto Protocol Zero's viruses and core. Exact counts ("hit 4 times") are not reliably followed ([Product docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/product/sound-effects/overview)). Independent testing of v3 found the following: a generic UI click came out soft until it was described as "a sharp, high plastic click"; vague prompts such as "spooky sound" came out over-processed; parts of multi-element prompts were dropped; and **output volume varied by 15–20 dB between clips**. The testers' advice is to generate small details separately and layer them, and to make 2–3 takes per sound ([AI for Mortals](https://www.aiformortals.co/blog/elevenlabs-sfx-v3)). For consistency, append a fixed style suffix to every prompt (for example, ", one-shot game sound effect, clean digital sci-fi, dry, no reverb, no music") and keep the model and prompt influence constant across the set. No official set-consistency guide exists, so this is derived practice. Example prompts in that pattern are "Short punchy square-wave blaster thunk with a burst of white noise, one-shot", "Crunchy bitcrushed digital pop as a computer virus is deleted, glitch, one-shot", and "Low electrical server-room drone with faint hard-drive clicks, seamless loop".

### Licensing

This is the part most likely to bite a student project later. The **Free plan has no commercial licence and requires crediting "elevenlabs.io" or "11.ai"**, and content generated outside a paid subscription can never be used commercially, even if you subscribe afterwards. **Content generated while on any paid plan can be used commercially indefinitely**, except output from Beta Services ([ElevenLabs help](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/help-center/legal/can-i-publish-the-content-i-generate-on-the-platform)). The Sound Effects terms explicitly allow use "in films, trailers, ads, games, and apps" when combined with other material. They **forbid selling or distributing the outputs as standalone files, samples or a sound library**, so shipping them inside the game build is fine but publishing the raw pack on itch.io is not ([SFX terms](https://elevenlabs.io/sound-effects-terms)). For a non-commercial course submission, Free plus a credit line is acceptable. If there is any chance of selling the game or entering it somewhere with prizes, spend $6 on one month of Starter and generate the final set then. This summary is not legal advice; check the live terms before release.

### Workflow from prompt to Godot

The pipeline that the sources support runs as follows. Keep the sound list (name, prompt, duration, loop flag, takes) in a CSV. Batch-generate with the official Python SDK, `client.text_to_sound_effects.convert(...)`, with the key in `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY` ([Cookbook](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/cookbooks/sound-effects)). The official MCP tool is fine for quick one-offs, but it caps duration at 5 s ([Glama listing](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/@elevenlabs/elevenlabs-mcp/tools/text_to_sound_effects)). Pick the best take or takes. Trim leading and trailing silence from one-shots, but never from `loop: true` files, because that breaks the loop point. Bring all clips to a common loudness reference, which matters given v3's level swings, then convert: one-shots to mono WAV and loops and music to OGG. Godot imports WAV, OGG and MP3 ([Godot import docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/assets_pipeline/importing_audio_samples.html)), but OGG supports only a loop-begin point, so set looping in the import dock. Finally, put the 2–3 kept takes of each frequent sound into an `AudioStreamRandomizer` and set final relative levels by ear in the bus mixer, checking the overall mix against the -24 LUFS PC target over a long play session. For music, Eleven Music (`POST /v1/music`, model `music_v2_5`, with `force_instrumental`) is paid-only and cleared for games, but it has **no loop parameter** ([Music API](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/music/compose); [Music capability](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/capabilities/music)). Layered stems for `AudioStreamSynchronized` will need manual editing, cut at bar boundaries at a stated BPM, or short beds made through the SFX endpoint with `loop: true`.

## Conclusion

The research points to one unglamorous takeaway: Protocol Zero's audio quality will depend less on how good any single sound is than on the rules around the sounds. That means a priority ladder topped by core damage and Overdrive, caps and cooldowns on the five or six sounds that fire constantly, randomizers on everything frequent, and a music system that escalates with threat. ElevenLabs removes the asset bottleneck that used to justify placeholder audio, so the scarce resource becomes design and tuning time. Its quirks (the deprecated default model, 15–20 dB level swings, and the Free-plan licensing trap) all need a decision made once at the start of the pipeline, not fixes later.

Two decisions should be made now because they constrain everything downstream. First, whether the submission ships as a Web build: if it does, avoid bus effects and design ducking and muffling as volume tweens. Second, whether the game could ever be monetized: if so, generate the final assets during a paid month. The most distinctive opportunity is thematic.
